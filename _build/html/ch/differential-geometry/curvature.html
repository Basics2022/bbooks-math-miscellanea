9.3. Curvature#

This section is devoted to curvature, and its different descriptions:

  • Extrinsic curvature, i.e. the description of the curvature of a \(n\)-manifold embedded in a \(n+1\)-space, as it’s usually done in the description of 2-dimensional surfaces in the 3-dimensional Euclidean space.

  • Intrinsic curvature, i.e. the description of a curvature of a \(n\)-manifold without the need for an embedding in a higher-dimensional space. This approach exploits the concepts of parallel transport, that - when applied to a vector field on a closed loop, see Parallel transport around a closed loop and the Riemann tensor - provides the definition of Riemann tensor, Definition 9.4. This is the common approach to Einstein General Relativity: spacetime is a 4-dimensional manifold, and it’s not embedded in a 5-dimensional space in the original formulation of GR theory.

9.3.1. Extrinsic curvature#

Extrinsic curvature of a \(n\)-dimensional manifold needs the manifold to be embedded in a \(n+1\)-dimensional space.

From the second order approximation of the \(n\)-dimensional manifold \(\mathbf{r}\left( q^i \right)\) in a \(n+1\)-dimensional space

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}\left( q^i + d q^i \right) - \mathbf{r}\left( q^i \right) & = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i} d q^i + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^k} d q^i d q^k + o(|d \mathbf{q}|^2) = \\ & = \mathbf{b}_i \, d q^i + \frac{1}{2} \Gamma_{ik}^{\alpha} \mathbf{b}_{\alpha} \, dq^i dq^k = \\ & = \mathbf{b}_i \, d q^i + \frac{1}{2} \left( \Gamma_{ik}^{l} \mathbf{b}_{l} + \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} \mathbf{n} \right) \, dq^i dq^k \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

with the Greek indices for sum over the \(n+1\) dimensions, and latin indices for the indices in \(\{ 1, \dots, n \}\). Here \(\mathbf{n} := \mathbf{b}_{n+1}\) s.t. \(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{b}_i = 0\), for \(n \in \{ 1, \dots, n \}\).

The extrinsic curvature tensor, already used for differential geometry of surfaces, is defined as \(\mathbb{b} := \Gamma^{n+1}_{ik} \mathbf{b}^i \otimes \mathbf{b}^k\).

Derivatives of the normal vector - Weingarten formula

The spatial derivatives of the unit normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}(q^i)\) reads

(9.4)#\[\frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial q^i} = -b_{i}^{\,\,l} \mathbf{b}_l \ .\]
Proof

Consider an \(n\)-dimensional hypersurface embedded in an \((n+1)\)-dimensional space with normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) satisfying \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{b}_i = 0\). The derivative of basis vectors decomposes into tangential and normal components:

\[\frac{\partial \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^k} = \Gamma^{\alpha}_{ik} \mathbf{b}_{\alpha} = \Gamma^l_{ik} \mathbf{b}_l + \Gamma^{n+1}_{ik} \hat{\mathbf{n}}\]

Differentiating the normality condition \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{b}_k = 0\) with respect to \(q^i\),

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} 0 & = \frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial q^i} \cdot \mathbf{b}_k + \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{b}_k}{\partial q^i} = \\ & = \frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial q^i} \cdot \mathbf{b}_k + \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \left( \Gamma^l_{ik} \mathbf{b}_l + \Gamma^{n+1}_{ik} \hat{\mathbf{n}} \right) \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

or

\[\frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial q^i} \cdot \mathbf{b}_k = -\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \left( \Gamma^l_{ik} \mathbf{b}_l + \Gamma^{n+1}_{ik} \hat{\mathbf{n}} \right) = -\Gamma^{n+1}_{ik}\]

From the latter expression, it immediately follows

\[\frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial q^i} = -\Gamma^{n+1}_{ik} \mathbf{b}^k = -g^{kl} \Gamma^{n+1}_{ik} \mathbf{b}_l = -b_{i}^{\,\,l} \mathbf{b}_l \ ,\]

where \(b_{ik} = \Gamma^{n+1}_{ik}\) is the second fundamental form.

9.3.2.1. Riemann curvature tensor#

9.3.2.1.1. Vector fields as differential operators, and directional or covariant derivatives#

A vector field \(X(f)\) acting on a scalar function \(f\) defines a directional derivative:

(9.5)#\[X(f) = X^\mu \partial_\mu f \ .\]

The definition of the vector field \(X\) as a differential operator can be recast as the scalar product of the vector field \(\mathbf{X}\) and the gradient of the function \(f\),

\[\nabla_{X} f = \mathbf{X} \cdot \nabla f \ .\]

The directional derivative of the function \(f\) along a curve \(\gamma\) with element \(d \mathbf{r}\) is \(d \mathbf{r} \cdot \nabla f\). So, for a scalar field \(f\),

\[\begin{aligned} d\mathbf{r} \cdot \nabla f & = \left( d q^\nu \mathbf{b}_\nu \right) \cdot \mathbf{b}^\mu \partial_{\mu} f = d q^\mu \partial_{\mu} f \ . \end{aligned}\]

For a vector field \(\mathbf{v}\),

(9.6)#\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} d\mathbf{r} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v} & = \left( d q^\nu \mathbf{b}_\nu \right) \cdot \mathbf{b}^\mu \partial_{\mu} \mathbf{v} = \\ & = d q^\mu \partial_{\mu} \mathbf{v} = \\ & = d q^{\mu} \left( \partial_{\mu} v^{\nu} + \Gamma_{\xi \mu}^{\nu} v^{\xi} \right) \mathbf{b}_{\nu} \ . \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

9.3.2.1.2. Parallel transport#

A vector field \(\mathbf{v}\) is parallel transported along a curve \(\gamma\) with element \(d\mathbf{r}\) if \(d \mathbf{r} \cdot \nabla f = \mathbf{0}\). Thus, using the relation (9.6), parallel transport implies

(9.7)#\[\begin{aligned} \partial_{\mu} v^{\nu} = - \Gamma_{\xi \mu}^{\nu} v^{\xi} \ . \end{aligned}\]

To first order, the transported vector \(v^\mu\) from \(q^\eta\) to \(q^\eta + dq^\eta\) satisfies:

\[v^\nu(q^\eta + d q^\eta) \sim v^\nu(q^\eta) + dq^\mu \partial_{\mu} v^\nu(q^\eta) = v^\nu(q^\eta) - dq^\mu \Gamma_{\mu \chi}^{\nu}(q^\eta) v^\chi(q^\eta)\ .\]

9.3.2.1.3. Lie bracket#

The Lie bracket of two vector fields \(X\) and \(Y\) measures their non-commutativity, i.e.

\[[X, Y]f = X(Y(f)) - Y(X(f)) \ .\]

In component notation, applying the definition (9.5) of a vector field as differential operator twice,

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left[ X, Y \right] f & = X^\mu \partial_\mu (Y^\nu \partial_\nu f) - Y^\nu \partial_\nu (X^\mu \partial_\mu f) = \\ & = X^\mu \partial_\mu Y^\nu \partial_\nu f + X^{\mu} Y^{\nu} \partial_{\mu \nu}f - Y^\nu \partial_\nu (X^\mu \partial_\mu f) - X^{\mu} Y^{\nu} \partial_{\mu \nu}f= \\ & = (X^\nu \partial_\nu Y^\mu - Y^\nu \partial_\nu X^\mu) \partial_\mu f \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

so that the components of the vector field \([X, Y]\) in the natural basis are

\[[X, Y]^\mu = X^\nu \partial_\nu Y^\mu - Y^\nu \partial_\nu X^\mu \ .\]

9.3.2.1.4. Parallel transport around a closed loop and the Riemann tensor#

9.3.2.1.4.1. Elementary loop#

The elementary loop is build with 2 vector fields \(X\), \(Y\). In general, the elementary loops need \(5\) sides if the vector fields \(X\), \(Y\) don’t commute. Let (todo Add some details, and pay attention to notation, as it can be non-trivial for curved spaces)

  • point A be in \(\mathbf{r}_A\)

  • point B be in \(\mathbf{r}_B = \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A)\)

  • point C be in \(\mathbf{r}_C = \mathbf{r}_B + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_B) = \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A))\)

  • point D be in \(\mathbf{r}_D = \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A)\)

  • point E be in \(\mathbf{r}_E = \mathbf{r}_D + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_D) = \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A) + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A))\)

The - maybe unexpected fifth side - is required when \(\mathbf{r}_E \ne \mathbf{r}_C\), i.e. when

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbf{0} \ne \mathbf{r}_E - \mathbf{r}_C & = \left\{ \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A) + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A) \cdot \nabla \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) \right\} + \\ & \quad - \left\{ \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) + \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A) + \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) \cdot \nabla \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A ) \right\} + o(|\mathbf{X}||\mathbf{Y}|) = \\ & = \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A) \cdot \nabla \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) - \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}_A) \cdot \nabla \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}_A) \ . \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

or, in components

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbf{0} \ne \mathbf{r}_E - \mathbf{r}_C & = Y^{\nu} \partial_{\nu} \left( X^{\mu} \mathbf{b}_{\mu} \right) - X^{\nu} \partial_{\nu} \left( Y^{\mu} \mathbf{b}_{\mu} \right) = \\ & = \left[ Y^{\nu} \left( \partial_{\nu} X^{\mu} + \Gamma_{\nu \xi}^{\mu} X^{\xi} \right) - X^{\nu} \left( \partial_{\nu} Y^{\mu} + \Gamma_{\nu \xi}^{\mu} Y^{\xi} \right) \right] \mathbf{b}_{\mu} = \\ & = - (X^\nu \partial_\nu Y^\mu - Y^\nu \partial_\nu X^\mu) \mathbf{b}_{\mu} = \\ & = - [ X, Y ]^{\mu} \mathbf{b}_{\mu} = - [ X, Y] \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

i.e. when \(X\), \(Y\) don’t commute, as stated above.

9.3.2.1.4.2. Parallel transport along a closed path#

Path A to C: first along \(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r})\) then along to \(\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}))\).

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}} & = \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ v^\nu - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - X^\alpha \left( \partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\phi} Y^\phi \right) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + \\ & \qquad - Y^\mu X^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + Y^\mu X^\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta + X^\alpha X^\mu (\partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\phi} Y^\phi) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta \Big] = \\ & \sim \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ v^\nu - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - X^\alpha \left( \partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\phi} Y^\phi \right) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + \\ & \qquad - Y^\mu X^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + Y^\mu X^\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta \Big] \end{aligned}\end{split}\]
Details

First transport along \(\mathbf{X}\), using (9.7),

\[\mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{b}_{\nu} \left( v^{\nu} + X^{\mu} \left\{ \partial_{\mu} v^{\nu} \right\}_{\parallel} \right) = \mathbf{b}_{\nu} \left( v^{\nu} - X^{\mu} \Gamma_{\mu \xi}^{\nu} v^{\xi} \right) \ .\]

and then along \(\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{X})\). Expanding \(\left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}}\) to second order in components:

\[\begin{aligned} \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}} & = \mathbf{b}_\nu \left[ v^\nu - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - \left( Y^\mu + X^\alpha (\partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\phi} Y^\phi) \right) \left( \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} + X^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \right) \left( v^\xi - X^\mu \Gamma^\xi_{\mu\eta} v^\eta \right) \right] \end{aligned}\]

Expanding terms explicitly:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}} & = \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ v^\nu - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - X^\alpha \left( \partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\phi} Y^\phi \right) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + \\ & \qquad - Y^\mu X^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + Y^\mu X^\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta + X^\alpha X^\mu (\partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\phi} Y^\phi) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta \Big] \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

A to C to E, to second order.

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left( \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}} \right)_{|| [Y,X]} & = \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ \left\{ \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}} \right\}^{\nu} + [ X, Y ]^{\mu} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu \xi} \left\{ \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{X}} \right)_{||\mathbf{Y}} \right\}^{\xi} \Big] = \\ & \sim \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ v^\nu - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - X^\alpha \left( \partial_\alpha Y^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\eta} Y^\eta \right) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + \\ & \qquad - Y^\mu X^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + Y^\mu X^\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta + \left( X^\alpha \partial_\alpha Y^{\mu} - Y^\alpha \partial_\alpha X^{\mu} \right) \Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu \xi} v^\xi \Big] \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Path A to E: first along \(\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r})\) then along to \(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{r}))\). Just swapping the \(X\), \(Y\)

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left( \mathbf{v}_{||\mathbf{Y}} \right)_{||\mathbf{X}} & = \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ v^\nu - Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - Y^\alpha \left( \partial_\alpha X^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\eta} X^\eta \right) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + \\ & \qquad - X^\mu Y^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + X^\mu Y^\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta + Y^\alpha Y^\mu (\partial_\alpha X^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\eta} X^\eta) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta \Big] = \\ & \sim \mathbf{b}_\nu \Big[ v^\nu - Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi - Y^\alpha \left( \partial_\alpha X^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\eta} X^\eta \right) \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + \\ & \qquad - X^\mu Y^\alpha \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi + X^\mu Y^\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} \Gamma^\xi_{\alpha\eta} v^\eta \Big] \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Difference path \(\text{A} \rightarrow \text{B} \rightarrow \text{C} \rightarrow \text{E}\) - path \(\text{A} \rightarrow \text{D} \rightarrow \text{E}\) to second order.

Subtracting Path B from Path A yields, as the travelling direction during the closed path is the opposite,:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} (A - B)^\nu & = \underbrace{ - X^\alpha \partial_\alpha Y^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi }_{ i_{[X,Y],2} } - \underbrace{X^\alpha Y^\beta \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi }_{ 1 } + \underbrace{ Y^\alpha \partial_\alpha X^\mu \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi }_{ i_{[X,Y],1} } + \underbrace{ X^\alpha Y^\beta \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha} \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\xi} v^\xi }_{ 1 } + \\ & \quad \ + \underbrace{ \left( X^\alpha \partial_\alpha Y^{\mu} - Y^\alpha \partial_\alpha X^{\mu} \right) \Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu \xi} v^\xi }_{ i_{[X,Y]} } + \\ & \quad \ + X^\alpha Y^\beta \Gamma^\nu_{\beta\eta} \Gamma^\eta_{\alpha\xi} v^\xi - X^\alpha Y^\beta \Gamma^\nu_{\alpha\eta} \Gamma^\eta_{\beta\xi} v^\xi - X^\alpha Y^\beta \partial_\alpha \Gamma^\nu_{\beta \xi} v^\xi + X^\alpha Y^\beta \partial_\beta \Gamma^\nu_{\alpha\xi} v^\xi + \text{higher order terms} = \\ & \sim - \left[ \partial_{\alpha} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\beta \xi} - \partial_{\beta} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha \xi} + \Gamma_{\alpha \eta}^{\nu} \Gamma_{\beta \xi}^{\eta} - \Gamma_{\beta \eta}^{\nu} \Gamma_{\alpha \xi}^{\eta} \right] X^{\alpha} Y^{\beta} v^{\xi} = \\ & = - v^\xi R^{\nu}_{\ \ \xi \alpha \beta \xi} X^\alpha Y^\beta \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

having introduced the definition of the Riemann tensor, \(R^{\nu}_{\ \ \alpha \beta \xi}\).

Definition 9.4 (Riemann tensor)

\[R^{\nu}_{\ \ \xi \alpha \beta} := \partial_{\alpha} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\beta \xi} - \partial_{\beta} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha \xi} + \Gamma_{\alpha \eta}^{\nu} \Gamma_{\beta \xi}^{\eta} - \Gamma_{\beta \eta}^{\nu} \Gamma_{\alpha \xi}^{\eta} \ . \]

Remark. The contribution of the Lie bracket, required to close the loop, annihilates with other two terms.

Remark. Exploiting the anti-symmetry of the Riemann tensor, \(R^{\nu}_{\ \ \alpha \beta \xi} = - R^{\nu}_{\ \ \beta \alpha \xi}\), it’s possible to recast the expression of the parallel transport of the vector field \(\mathbf{v}\) along the elementary closed path \(\gamma_{X,Y}\) built with the vector fields \(X\), \(Y\) as

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} H^\nu = \Delta v^{nu} & := \oint_{\gamma_{X,Y}}d q^{\mu} \left\{ \partial_{\mu} v^{\nu} \right\}_{||} = \\ & := - \oint_{\gamma_{X,Y}} v^{\xi} \, \Gamma_{\mu \xi}^{\nu} \, d q^{\mu} = \\ & = - \frac{1}{2} v^{\xi} \, R^{\nu}_{\ \ \xi \alpha \beta} \left( X^{\alpha} Y^{\beta} - X^{\beta} Y^{\alpha} \right) + \text{higher order terms} = \\ & = - \frac{1}{2} v^{\xi} \, R^{\nu}_{\ \ \xi \alpha \beta} \, A^{\alpha \beta} + \text{higher order terms} \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

having introduced the signed areas \(A^{\alpha \beta}\) of the projection of the area enclosed in the elementary loop onto the plane defined by the coordinates \(q^{\alpha}\) and \(q^{\beta}\).

Riemann Curvature Tensor Definition

For arbitrary vector fields \(X, Y, Z\):

\[R(X, Y)Z = \nabla_X \nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y \nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X, Y]} Z\]

If \(X, Y\) are coordinate vector fields (\([X, Y] = 0\)), this simplifies to:

9.3.2.2. Relation between Riemann curvature tensor and extrinsic curvature#

If the parametrization of the space is regular enough, second-order mixed derivatives have the same value, i.e. \(\tfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^k} = \tfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^k \partial q^i}\) that implies \(\Gamma_{ik}^{\alpha} = \Gamma_{ki}^{\alpha}\). Similar relation holds for the mixed derivatives of vector \(\mathbf{b}_i\) of the natural basis, \(\tfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^j \partial q^k} = \tfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^k \partial q^j}\). As

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^j \partial q^k} & = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial q^j} \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^k} = \\ & = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial q^j} \left( \Gamma_{ik}^{\alpha} \mathbf{b}_{\alpha} \right) = \\ & = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial q^j} \left( \Gamma_{ik}^{l} \mathbf{b}_{l} + \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} \mathbf{n} \right) = \\ & = \partial_j \Gamma^{l}_{ik} \mathbf{b}_{l} + \Gamma_{ik}^{l} \left( \Gamma_{l j}^{m} \mathbf{b}_{m} + \Gamma_{l j}^{n+1} \mathbf{n} \right) + \partial_j \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} \mathbf{n} + \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} \partial_j \mathbf{n} = \\ & = \left( \partial_j \Gamma_{ik}^l + \Gamma_{ik}^m \Gamma_{mj}^l - \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} b^l_j \right) \mathbf{b}_l + \left( \partial_j \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} + \Gamma_{ik}^{l} \Gamma_{lj}^{n+1} \right) \mathbf{n} \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

having used the Weingarten equation (9.4), \(\partial_j \mathbf{n} = - g^{kl} b_{jk} \mathbf{b}_l = - b^{\ \ l}_j \mathbf{b}_l\).

Symmetries of \(\ \mathbb{b}\)

Since \(b_{ij} := \Gamma^{n+1}_{ij}\), then these symbols are symmetric \(b_{ij} = b_{ji}\). Since \(b_{i}^{\ \ l} = b_{ij} g^{jl}\), exploiting the symmetries of the symbols \(b_{ij}\) and \(g^{kl}\), it follows

\[b_{i}^{\ \ l} = b_{ij} g^{jl} = b_{ji} g^{jl} = b^{l}_{\ \ i} \ .\]

Equating every tangential components of \(\partial_{jk} \mathbf{b}_i\) and \(\partial_{kj} \mathbf{b}_i\), it follows for the component \(l\),

\[ \partial_j \Gamma_{ik}^l + \Gamma_{ik}^m \Gamma_{mj}^l - \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} b^l_j = \partial_k \Gamma_{ij}^l + \Gamma_{ij}^m \Gamma_{mk}^l - \Gamma_{ij}^{n+1} b^l_k \ , \]

or, rearranging

\[ \underbrace{\partial_j \Gamma_{ik}^l - \partial_k \Gamma_{ij}^l + \Gamma_{ik}^m \Gamma_{mj}^l - \Gamma_{ij}^m \Gamma_{mk}^l}_{ = R^{l}_{\ \ ijk} } = \Gamma_{ik}^{n+1} b^l_j - \Gamma_{ij}^{n+1} b^l_k \ , \]

having introduced the definition of Riemann curvature tensor \(R^{l}_{\ \ ijk}\). Writing \(\Gamma^{n+1}_{ij} = b_{ij}\), as done in (9.1) dealing with curvature and second fundamental form, it follows

(9.13)#\[R^{l}_{\ \ ijk} = b_{ik} b^l_j - b_{ij} b^l_k \]

See also General Relativity: Differential Geometry: Curvature Tensor

9.3.2.2.1. Riemann tensor for 2-dimensional surfaces#

\[R_{mijk} = g_{ml} R^{l}_{\ \ ijk} = g_{ml} \left( b_{ik} b^l_j - b_{ij} b^l_k \right) = b_{ik} b_{jm} - b_{ij} b_{mk} \ . \]

For a 2-dimensional surface, indices belong to the set \(\{ 1, 2 \}\). In general, it has 16 components. Exploiting symmetries of the Riemann tensor, it’s possible to find a smaller set of independent components. Property (9.8), \(R_{mijk} = -R_{mikj}\), sets all the elements with \(k = j\) to zero, i.e. 8 components are set to zero. Only 4 of the remaining components could be independent. Let’s say we choose the - possibly - independent components to be the one with \(j = 1\), \(k = 2\), i.e.

\[\left\{ R_{1112}, R_{1212}, R_{2112}, R_{2212} \right\}\]

Property (9.9), \(R_{mijk} = -R_{imjk}\), sets the elements \(R_{mijk}\) with \(m = i\) to zero, and thus the number of possibly independent components becomes 2, since \(R_{1112} = R_{2212} = 0\). The same property implies that \(R_{2112} = - R_{1212}\), so the number of possibly independent components becomes 1, usually chosen as \(R_{1212}\).

Thus, non-zero components are

\[R_{1212} = - R_{2112} = - R_{1221} = R_{2121} \ .\]

Using (9.13), it can be proved that the component \(R_{1212}\) is equal to the determinant of the second fundamental form \(\mathbb{b}\),

\[R_{1212} = b_{22} b_{11} - b_{12}^2 = L N - M^2 = K g \ .\]

having introduced the Gaussian curvature, using its expression as the ratio of the determinant of the second and first fundamental form (9.2), \(K = \frac{\text{det}\left\{ b_{ij} \right\} }{\text{det}\left\{ g_{ij} \right\} } = \frac{LN - M^2}{g}\).

Ricci tensor. The components of Ricci tensor, \(R_{ik} := g^{mj} R_{mijk}\), are

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} R_{11} & = g^{mj} R_{m1j1} = g^{22} R_{2121} = \ \ g^{22} K g = g_{11} K \\ R_{12} & = g^{mj} R_{m1j2} = g^{21} R_{2112} = - g^{21} K g = g_{12} K \\ R_{21} & = g^{mj} R_{m2j1} = g^{12} R_{1221} = - g^{12} K g = g_{21} K \\ R_{22} & = g^{mj} R_{m2j2} = g^{11} R_{1212} = \ \ g^{11} K g = g_{22} K \ ,\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

i.e. Ricci tensor of a 2-dimensional surface is proportional to the metric tensor, and the proportionality constant is the Gaussian curvature,

\[R_{ij} = K g_{ij} \ .\]

Scalar curvature. The scalar curvature, \(R := g^{ij} R_{ij}\), is simply \(R = 2 K\).

9.3.2.3. Examples#

Example 9.2 (2-d Euclidean space is flat, independently from the set of coordinates used for the description)

While it’s trivial to prove that the 2-dimensional Euclidean space is flat using Cartesian coordinates, in this result is also proved using polar coordinates. This should be an expected result, as an intrinsic/tensor relation holds independently from an arbitrary choice of coordinates or vector basis.

Cartesian coordinates. Cartesian coordinates have uniform (constant in space) vectors of the natural basis, and thus all the Christoffel symbols equal to zero - being the components of their space derivatives - and the partial derivatives of the Christoffell symbols equal to zero as well. Thus,

\[R^{k}_{\ m i j} = 0 \ .\]
Polar coordinates

**Polar Coordinates \((r, \theta)\). Let \(q^1 = r\), \(q^2 = \theta\). Thus, the natural basis and its reciprocal - using the common unit-length orhogonal polar basis - are

\[\begin{split} \left\{ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{b}_1 & = \partial_1 \mathbf{r} = \hat{r} \\ \mathbf{b}_2 & = \partial_2 \mathbf{r} = r \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} \end{aligned}\right. \quad , \quad \left\{ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{b}^1 & = \hat{r} \\ \mathbf{b}^2 & = \frac{1}{r} \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} \end{aligned}\right. \ .\end{split}\]

Metric tensor, \(g_{ij} = \mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j\).

\[\begin{split}g_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & r^2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad g^{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{r^2} \end{pmatrix}\end{split}\]

Non-vanishing Christoffel Symbols. Using \(\Gamma^l_{ik} = \frac{1}{2} g^{lj} \left( \partial_i g_{jk} + \partial_k g_{ji} - \partial_j g_{ik} \right)\):

  1. \(\Gamma^1_{22} = \frac{1}{2} g^{11} (-\partial_1 g_{22}) = \frac{1}{2}(1)(-2r) = -r\)

  2. \(\Gamma^2_{12} = \Gamma^2_{21} = \frac{1}{2} g^{22} (\partial_1 g_{22}) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r^2}\right)(2r) = \frac{1}{r}\)

All other \(\Gamma^k_{ij} = 0\).

Non-vanishing derivatives of Christoffel Symbols.

  • \(\partial_1 \Gamma^2_{12} = \partial_r \left(\frac{1}{r}\right) = -\frac{1}{r^2}\)

  • \(\partial_1 \Gamma^1_{22} = \partial_r (-r) = -1\)

Calculation of Riemann Tensor Components. Riemann tensor of a 2-dimensional manifold has 16 components. Not all the components are independent Exploiting symmetries of the Riemann tensor, \(R^k_{\,m i j} = \partial_i \Gamma^k_{j m} - \partial_j \Gamma^k_{i m} + \Gamma^l_{j m} \Gamma^k_{i l} - \Gamma^l_{i m} \Gamma^k_{j l}\), it’s easy to realized that

\[R^{k}_{\ m i j} = - R^{k}_{\ m j i} \ .\]

Thus, all the components with \(i = j\) are identically zero. So, only 4 components can be independent, setting as an example \(i = 1\), \(j = 2\), and evaluating the 4 terms with \((k,m) \in \{ 1 , 2 \}^2\).

Derivatives of the components of the metric tensor

Let \(g_{ij} = \mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j\), \(\mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}^j = \delta_{i}^{j}\) and \(\partial_{k} \mathbf{b}_i = \Gamma_{ik}^{l} \mathbf{b}_l\), then

\[\begin{aligned} \partial_{k} g_{ij} & = \partial_k \mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j + \mathbf{b}_i \cdot \partial_k \mathbf{b}_j = \Gamma_{ik}^{l} g_{lj} + \Gamma_{ik}^{l} g_{il} \ . \end{aligned}\]

Thus, swapping indices

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \partial_{k} g_{ij} & = \Gamma_{ik}^{l} g_{lj} + \Gamma_{jk}^{l} g_{il} \\ \partial_{i} g_{jk} & = \Gamma_{ji}^{l} g_{lk} + \Gamma_{ki}^{l} g_{jl} \\ \partial_{j} g_{ki} & = \Gamma_{kj}^{l} g_{li} + \Gamma_{ij}^{l} g_{kl} \\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

From the latter three relations, it’s possible to retrieve the expression of Christoffel coefficients as a function of the components of the metric tensor and their derivatives. As an example, coefficient \(\Gamma_{ik}^{l}\) is found summing the first and the second relation, and subtracting the third one,

\[\Gamma_{ik}^l g_{jl} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \partial_{k} g_{ij} + \partial_{i} g_{jk} - \partial_{j} g_{ki} \right) \ ,\]

or

\[\Gamma_{ik}^l = \frac{1}{2} g^{lj} \left( \partial_{k} g_{ij} + \partial_{i} g_{jk} - \partial_{j} g_{ki} \right) \ .\]

Example 9.3 (Parallel transport of a vector on a sphere)

Coordinates and metric tensor

Using spherical coordinates \(\left(q^1, q^2 \right) = (\theta, \phi)\),1 the metric tensor reads

\[\begin{split} \left[ g_{ij} \right] = \begin{bmatrix} R^2 \sin^2 q^2 & \cdot \\ \cdot & R^2 \end{bmatrix} \quad , \quad \left[ g^{ij} \right] = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{R^2 \sin^2 q^2} & \cdot \\ \cdot & \frac{1}{R^2} \end{bmatrix} \ ,\end{split}\]

with constant \(R\), i.e. the radius of the sphere.

Christoffel symbols

The only non-zero derivative of the components of the metric tensor is

\[\partial_{2} g_{11} = 2 R^2 \sin q^2 \cos q^2 \ .\]

Non-zero Christoffel symbols are

  • with upper index = 1:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{ik}^1 & = \frac{1}{2} g^{1 j} \left( \partial_k g_{ij} + \partial_{i} g_{jk} - \partial_j g_{ki} \right) = && \left( j = 1, \ g^{11} = \frac{1}{R^2 \sin^2 q^2} \right) \\ & = \frac{1}{2} g^{1 1} \left( \partial_k g_{i1} + \partial_{i} g_{1k} - \partial_1 g_{ki} \right) = && \left( \partial_1 g_{ik} = 0 \right) \\ & = \frac{1}{2} g^{1 1} \left( \partial_k g_{i1} + \partial_i g_{1k} \right) \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    then

    \[\Gamma_{12}^{1} = \Gamma_{21}^{1} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{R^2 \sin^2 q^2} 2 R^2 \sin q^2 \cos q^2 = \frac{\cos q^2}{\sin q^2} \ .\]

  • with upper index = 2:

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{ik}^2 & = \frac{1}{2} g^{2 j} \left( \partial_k g_{ij} + \partial_{i} g_{jk} - \partial_j g_{ki} \right) = && \left( j = 2, \ g^{22} = \frac{1}{R^2} \right) \\ & = \frac{1}{2} g^{22} \left( \partial_k g_{i2} + \partial_{i} g_{2k} - \partial_2 g_{ki} \right) = && \left( \partial g_{2 \cdot} = 0 , \ \partial_{2} g_{11} = 2 R^2 \sin q^2 \cos q^2 \right) \\ & = - \frac{1}{2} g^{22} \partial_{2} g_{ik} \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    then

    \[\Gamma_{11}^{2} = - \sin q^2 \cos q^2 \ .\]

Non-zero derivatives of the Christoffel symbols

\[\partial_2 \Gamma_{12}^1 = \partial_2 \Gamma_{21}^1 = - \frac{1}{\sin^2 q^2} \ ,\]
\[\partial_2 \Gamma_{11}^2 = \sin^2 q^2 - \cos^2 q^2 \ .\]
Parallel transport along a meridian, \(\ \theta = \text{const.}\), i.e. \(\ q^1 = \text{const.}\)

A meridian can be parametrized with \(q^2 = \phi\) as a parameter, as the coordinate \(q^1 = \theta\) is constant, and thus \(dq^1 = 0\). The parallel transport of the vector field thus becomes a system of 2 equations in 2 unknowns \(v^1(q^2)\), \(v^2(q^2)\), namely

\[\begin{split}\left\{\begin{aligned} d_2 v^1 & = - \Gamma_{12}^1 v^1 - \Gamma_{22}^1 v^2 = - \text{cot}\left( q^2 \right) v^1( q^2 ) \\ d_2 v^2 & = - \Gamma_{12}^2 v^1 - \Gamma_{22}^2 v^2 = 0 \\ \end{aligned}\right.\end{split}\]

Thus the solution reads

\[\begin{split}\left\{ \begin{aligned} v^1( \phi ) & = v^1_0 \frac{\sin \phi_0}{\sin \phi} \\ v^2( \phi ) & = v^2_0 \\ \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

Details. Thus \(v_2\left( q^2 \right) = \overline{v^2}\), and \(v^1\) can be evaluated with separation of variables,

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \int_{v_1}^{v_2} \frac{d v}{v} & = - \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \text{cot}(x) \, dx \\ \left.\ln \left( v \right)\right|_{v_1}^{v_2} & = - \left.\ln \left( \sin \phi \right)\right|_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2} \\ \frac{v_2}{v_1} & = \frac{ \sin \phi_1 }{ \sin \phi_2 } \\ v_2 \sin \phi_2 & = v_1 \sin \phi_1 \ . \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

being

\[\int \text{cot}(x) dx = \int \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} dx = \int \frac{d \sin x}{\sin x} = \ln \left( \sin x \right) \ .\]

Remark. The first equation of the system can be written as \(v'(x) = - \frac{\cos(x)}{\sin(x)} v(x)\), or

\[0 = v'(x) \sin(x) + v(x) \cos(x) = \tfrac{d}{dx} \left( v(x) \sin(x) \right)\ .\]
Parallel transport along a parallel, \(\ \phi = \text{const.}\), i.e. \(\ q^2 = \text{const.}\)

A parallel can be parametrized with \(q^1 = \theta\) as a parameter, as the coordinate \(q^2 = \overline{\phi}\) is constant, and thus \(dq^2 = 0\). The parallel transport of the vector field thus becomes a system of 2 equations in 2 unknowns \(v^1(q^1)\), \(v^2(q^1)\), namely

\[\begin{split}\left\{\begin{aligned} d_1 v^1 (q^1) & = - \Gamma_{11}^1 v^1 - \Gamma_{21}^1 v^2 = - \text{cot}\left( q^2 \right) v^2 (q^1) \\ d_1 v^2 (q^1) & = - \Gamma_{11}^2 v^1 - \Gamma_{21}^2 v^2 = \sin\left( q^2 \right) \cos\left( q^2 \right) \, v^1 (q^1) \\ \end{aligned}\right.\end{split}\]

As \(q^2 = \overline{\phi}\) is constant,

\[\begin{split}\left\{ \begin{aligned} v^1( \theta ) & = v_1 \frac{\sin \phi_1}{\sin \phi} \\ v^2( \theta ) & = 0 \\ \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

Details.

\[\begin{split}\left\{ \begin{aligned} \frac{d v^{1}}{d \theta} (\theta) & = - \frac{\cos(\phi)}{\sin(\phi)} \, v^2(\theta) \\ \frac{d v^{2}}{d \theta} (\theta) & = \sin(\phi) \cos(\phi) \, v^1(\theta) \\ \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

Taking the derivative \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\) of the first equation, and replacing \(\frac{d v^2}{d \theta}\) with the second equation,

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} 0 & = \left( v^1 \right)'' + \frac{\cos(\phi)}{\sin(\phi)} \sin(\phi) \cos(\phi) \, v^1(\theta) = \\ & = \left( v^1 \right)''(\theta) + \cos^2(\phi) \, v^1(\theta) \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

and thus

  • if \(\cos(\phi) \ne 0\)

    \[\begin{split}\left\{ \begin{aligned} v^1(\theta) & = A \cos\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) + B \sin\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) \\ v^2(\theta) & = - \frac{\sin(\phi)}{\cos(\phi)} \left[ - A \cos(\phi) \sin\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) + B \cos(\phi) \cos\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) \right] \\ & = A \sin(\phi) \sin\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) - B \sin(\phi) \cos\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) \\ \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

    Choosing the reference of \(\theta\), to be \(\theta = 0\) at the beginning of the path, then the initial conditions simplify to

    \[\begin{split}\left\{ \begin{aligned} v^1_0 & = A \\ v^2_0 & = - \sin(\phi) B \ , \end{aligned} \right. \quad \rightarrow \quad \left\{ \begin{aligned} A & = v^1_0 \\ B & = - \frac{v^2_0}{\sin \phi} \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

  • if \(\cos(\phi) = 0\), then \(v^1(\theta) = v^1_0\), \(v^2(\theta) = v^2_0\) constant.

Parallel transport from the equator to the pole and back

Let a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) on the equator and parallel to the local meridian, i.e. \(\mathbf{v} = v^\phi \mathbf{b}_2\) in \((\theta, \phi) = \left( 0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\).

1. Parallel transport along the equator, \(\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}\), \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(\overline{\theta}\). The equator is described by the coordinate \(\phi = \frac{\pi}{2}\), and thus \(\cos \phi = 0\). The solution of the parallel transport equation on the equator gives constant \(v^1(\theta)\) and \(v^2(\theta)\). Thus,

\[v^1\left(\overline{\theta}\right) = 0 \quad , \quad v^2\left(\overline{\theta}\right) = v^\phi \ .\]

2. Parallel transport along the meridian, \(\theta = \overline{\theta}\), \(\phi\) from \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) to \(\delta \phi\).

\[v^1\left( 0 \right) = 0 \quad , \quad v^2\left(\overline{\theta}\right) = v^\phi \ .\]

3. Parallel transport along a small circle around the pole to avoid singularity.

\[\begin{split}\left\{ \begin{aligned} v^1(\theta) & = v^1_0 \cos\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) - \frac{v^2_0}{\sin \phi} \sin\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) = \\ & = - \frac{v^\phi}{\sin \delta \phi} \sin\left( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta\right) \\ v^2(\theta) & = v^1_0 \sin(\phi) \sin\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) + v^2_0 \cos\left( \cos(\phi) \theta\right) = \\ & = v^\phi \cos\left( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta\right) \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

The final values are \(v^1 = \frac{v^\phi}{\sin \delta \phi} \sin( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta )\), \(v^2 = v^\phi \cos( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta )\). Exchanging the reference value for \(\theta\) should be enough. todo check!

4. Parallel transport along the meridian, \(\theta = 0\), \(\phi\) from \(\delta \phi\) to \(\frac{\pi}{2}\).

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} v^1\left( 0 \right) & = \frac{\sin(\delta \phi)}{\sin( \pi / 2)} \frac{v^\phi}{\sin \delta \phi} \sin( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta ) = \\ & = v^\phi \sin( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta ) \\ & \rightarrow v^\phi \sin( \theta ) \quad \text{for $\delta \phi \rightarrow 0$} \\ v^2\left( 0 \right) & = v^\phi \cos( \cos(\delta \phi) \theta ) \\ & \rightarrow v^{\phi} \cos( \theta ) \quad \text{for $\delta \phi \rightarrow 0$} \ . \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Result. The final vector after the parallel transport over the closed path is the initial vector rotated by the angle \(\theta\).

Example 9.4 (Riemann tensor on the 2-dimensional sphere)

With \(\left( q^1, q^2 \right) = \left( \theta, \phi \right)\),

\[ \Gamma_{12}^{1} = \Gamma_{21}^{1} = \frac{\cos \phi}{\sin \phi} \quad , \quad \Gamma_{11}^{2} = - \sin \phi \cos \phi \]
\[ \partial_2 \Gamma_{12}^{1} = \partial_2 \Gamma_{21}^{1} = - \frac{1}{\sin^2 \phi} \quad , \quad \partial_2 \Gamma_{11}^{2} = \sin^2 \phi - \cos^2 \phi \]

Riemann tensor,

\[R^{\nu}_{\ \ \alpha \beta \xi} := \partial_{\alpha} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\beta \xi} - \partial_{\beta} \Gamma^{\nu}_{\alpha \xi} + \Gamma_{\alpha \eta}^{\nu} \Gamma_{\beta \xi}^{\eta} - \Gamma_{\beta \eta}^{\nu} \Gamma_{\alpha \xi}^{\eta} \ . \]

with the symmetry \(R^{\nu}_{\ \ \alpha \beta \xi} = - R^{\nu}_{\ \ \beta \alpha \xi}\), for a 2-dimensional manifold there’s need to evaluate only 4 components (are all these 4 components independent?)

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} R^{\mathbf{1}}_{\ \ 12 \mathbf{1}} & = 0 \\ R^{\mathbf{1}}_{\ \ 12 \mathbf{2}} & = -\partial_2 \Gamma_{12}^{1} + \Gamma^{1}_{21} \Gamma^{1}_{12} = \frac{1}{\sin^2 \phi} - \frac{\cos^2 \phi}{\sin^2 \phi} = \frac{1}{\sin^2 \phi} \\ R^{\mathbf{2}}_{\ \ 12 \mathbf{1}} & = - \partial_{2} \Gamma_{11}^{2} + \Gamma^2_{11} \Gamma_{21}^{1} = - \sin^2 \phi + \cos^2 \phi - \cos^2\phi = - \sin^2 \phi \\ R^{\mathbf{2}}_{\ \ 12 \mathbf{2}} & = 0 \\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} R_{1122} & = g_{11} R^{1}_{\ \ 122} = \frac{R^2 \sin^2 \phi}{\sin^2 \phi} = R^2 \\ R_{2122} & = g_{22} R^{2}_{\ \ 121} = - R^2 \sin^2 \phi \ . \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Extrinsic approach. Recalling that \(\mathbf{r} = r \hat{\mathbf{r}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}} \left( r \sin \phi \cos \theta \right) + \hat{\mathbf{y}} \left( r \sin \phi \sin \theta \right) + \hat{\mathbf{z}} r \cos \phi\), thus \(\mathbf{b}_{1} = \partial_{\theta} \mathbf{r} = -r \sin \phi \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{x}} + r \sin \phi \cos \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{y}}\), and \(\mathbf{b}_2 = \partial_{\phi} \mathbf{r} = r \cos \phi \cos \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{x}} + r \cos \phi \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{y}} - r \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf{z}}\). The covariant components of the metric tensor are \(g_{11} = r^2 \sin^2 \phi\), \(g_{12} = 0\), \(g_{22} = r^2\).
