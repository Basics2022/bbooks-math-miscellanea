9.2. Surfaces#

A surface \(S\) is a geometric entity that has a 2-parameter parametric representation. Its points in 3-dimensional (Euclidean) space \(E^3\) can be represented with the function

\[S: \, \mathbf{r}(q^1, q^2) \ .\]

9.2.1. Differential#

The differential \(d \mathbf{r}\) represents an elementary vector on - tangent to - the surface. From the very definition of the differential of a function, it follows

\[d \mathbf{r} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^1} \, d q^1 + \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^2} \, d q^2 = \mathbf{b}_1 \, d q^1 + \mathbf{b}_2 \, d q^2 \]

9.2.2. Normal vector#

The vectors \(\mathbf{b}_1\), \(\mathbf{b}_2\) are tangent to the surface \(S\). A regular parametrization has \(\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2 \ne \mathbf{0}\) everywhere. A third vector \(\mathbf{b}_3 := \hat{\mathbf{n}}\) can be defined so that \(|\hat{\mathbf{n}}| = 1\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{b}_{i} = 0\), \(i=1:2\). This is a unit normal vector to the surface. If the parametrization is regular, then the set of vectors \(\{ \mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \hat{\mathbf{n}} \}\) forms a local(natural) basis of 3-dimensional vectors space.

The components of the metric tensor are

\[\begin{split}\left[ g_{ab} \right] = \begin{bmatrix} g_{11} & g_{12} & \cdot \\ g_{21} & g_{22} & \cdot \\ \cdot & \cdot & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ ,\end{split}\]

so that the vectors of the reciprocal basis, \(\mathbf{b}^a = g^{ab} \mathbf{b}_b\), read

\[\begin{split}\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{b}^1 & = g^{11} \mathbf{b}_1 + g^{12} \mathbf{b}_2 \\ \mathbf{b}^2 & = g^{21} \mathbf{b}_1 + g^{22} \mathbf{b}_2 \\ \mathbf{b}^3 & = \hat{\mathbf{n}} \ . \end{aligned} \right.\end{split}\]

9.2.3. Derivatives of the natural basis#

For \(i=1:2\), \(j=1:2\)

\[\frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^j} = \Gamma_{ij}^k \mathbf{b}_k = \Gamma_{ij}^1 \mathbf{b}_1 + \Gamma_{ij}^2 \mathbf{b}_2 + \Gamma_{ij}^3 \mathbf{b}_3 \ ,\]

so that

\[\Gamma_{ij}^{k} = \mathbf{b}^k \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{b}_i}{\partial q^j} \ .\]

Remark. Comparing two different choices of the orthogonal coordinate \(q^3\), (1) unit normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}_3 = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\), (2) non-unitary normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}_3 = \mathbf{n}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}^3 = \tfrac{\mathbf{n}}{|\mathbf{n}|^2}\), it’s easy to prove that the Christoffel coefficients with upper index \(k = 3\), \(\Gamma^{3}_{ij}\), are independent from this choice.

9.2.4. Geometric entities#

Normal vector.

\[\mathbf{n}(q^1, q^2) = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^1}(q^1, q^2) \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^2}(q^1, q^2) = \mathbf{b}_1(q^1, q^2) \times \mathbf{b}_2(q^1, q^2)\]

First-order approximation and the tangent plane. The equation of the points of the tangent plane \(\pi\) in point \(\mathbf{r}\left(q^1, q^2 \right)\) follows the orthogonality condition

\[\pi: \, (\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}(q^1, q^2)) \cdot \mathbf{n}(q^1, q^2) = 0 \ .\]

Length of elementary segment on the plane.

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} |d \mathbf{r}|^2 & = d \mathbf{r} \cdot d \mathbf{r} = \\ & = \left( \mathbf{b}_1 \, d q^1 + \mathbf{b}_2 \, d q^2 \right) \cdot \left( \mathbf{b}_1 \, d q^1 + \mathbf{b}_2 \, d q^2 \right) = \\ & = g_{11} \, dq^1 \, dq^1 + g_{12} \, dq^1 \, dq^2 + g_{21} \, dq^2 \, dq^1 + g_{22} \, dq^2 \, d q^2 = g_{ij} \, dq^i \, dq^j \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Second order approximation and the curvature tensor.

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}(q^1 + d q^1, q^2 + d q^2) & = \mathbf{r}(q_1, q_2) + \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i} \, dq^i + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} \, dq^i dq^j + o\left(|d q^i|^2\right) = \\ & = \mathbf{r}(q_1, q_2) + \mathbf{b}_{i} \, dq^i + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{b}_k \Gamma^{k}_{ij} \, dq^i \, dq^j + \frac{1}{2} \hat{\mathbf{n}} \, \Gamma^{3}_{ij} \, dq^i dq^j + o\left(|d q^i|^2\right) \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

so that

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \left[ \mathbf{r}(q^1 + d q^1, q^2 + d q^2) - \mathbf{r}(q^1, q^2) \right] \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} & = \frac{1}{2} \Gamma^3_{ij} \, d q^i \, dq^j = \\ & = \frac{1}{2} \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} \, d q^i \, dq^j = \\ & = \frac{1}{2} \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} \, \mathbf{b}^i \cdot \mathbf{b}_k d q^k \, \mathbf{b}^j \cdot \mathbf{b}_l dq^l = \\ & = \frac{1}{2} \underbrace{d q^k \mathbf{b}_k}_{d \mathbf{r}} \cdot \left[ \left( \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} \right) \mathbf{b}^i \otimes \mathbf{b}^j \right] \cdot \underbrace{d q^l \mathbf{b}_l}_{d\mathbf{r}} = \\ & = \frac{1}{2} d \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbb{b} \cdot d \mathbf{r} \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

having defined the curvature tensor \(\mathbb{b}\),

\[\mathbb{b} = b_{ij} \mathbf{b}^i \otimes \mathbf{b}^j = \left( \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} \right) \mathbf{b}^i \otimes \mathbf{b}^j \ .\]

9.2.4.1. Curvature tensor#

From the second-order Taylor expansion around a point \(\mathbf{r}(q^1, q^2)\), the projection of the displacement onto the unit normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) measures the distance from the surface to its tangent plane \(\pi\):

\[[\mathbf{r}(q^1 + dq^1, q^2 + dq^2) - \mathbf{r}(q^1, q^2)] \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{1}{2} \Gamma_{ij}^3 dq^i dq^j = \frac{1}{2} b_{ij} dq^i dq^j .\]

The symmetric covariant tensor of rank 2, defined by its components

\[b_{ij} := \Gamma_{ij}^3 = \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{r}}{\partial q^i \partial q^j} = -\frac{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial q^i} \cdot \mathbf{b}_j ,\]

is the second fundamental form (or curvature tensor) of the surface embedded in \(E^3\).

The quadratic form \(b_{ij} dq^i dq^j\) characterizes the local deviation of the surface from the tangent plane \(\pi\). Let \(w_n(q^1,q^2) = d \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbb{b} \cdot d \mathbf{r}\) the normal deviation of the surface w.r.t. the tangent plane, then

\[w_n(q^1,q^2) = b_{ij} dq^{i} dq^{j} \ .\]

The curvature tensor \(\mathbb{b}\) is a symmetric order-2 tensor, locally built on a 2-dimensional vector space. Its spectral decomposition

\[\mathbb{b} = \kappa_1 \mathbf{v}_1 \otimes \mathbf{v}_1 + \kappa_2 \mathbf{v}_2 \otimes \mathbf{v}_2 \ ,\]

being \(\kappa_1\), \(\kappa_2 \in \mathbb{R}\) the real egivenvalues, and \(\mathbf{v}_1\), \(\mathbf{v}_2\) the unit orthogonal basis defined by the eigenvectors of the eigenvalue problem

\[\mathbb{b} \cdot \mathbf{v}_i = \kappa_i \mathbf{v}_i \ , \qquad i \in \{ 1, 2 \} \ .\]

The eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_{1,2}\) defines the principal directions, while the eigenvalues \(\kappa_{1,2}\) are defined as the principal curvatures.

Eigenvalue problem
\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbb{b} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{(\alpha)} & = \kappa_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{v}_{(\alpha)} \\ \left( b_{ij} \mathbf{b}^i \otimes \mathbf{b}^j \right) \cdot \left( v^l_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_l \right) & = \kappa_{(\alpha)} v^m_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_m \\ b_{ij} v^j_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}^i & = \kappa_{(\alpha)} v^m_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_m \\ g^{im} b_{ij} v^j_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_m & = \kappa_{(\alpha)} v^m_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_m \\ b^{m}_{\ \ j} v^j_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_m & = \kappa_{(\alpha)} v^m_{(\alpha)} \mathbf{b}_m \\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

i.e. in components \(b^{m}_{\ \ j} v^{j}_{(\alpha)} = \kappa_{(\alpha)} v^m_{(\alpha)}\), or

\[\left( b^{m}_{\ \ j} - \kappa_{(\alpha)} \delta^{m}_{\ \ j} \right) v^{j}_{(\alpha)} = 0 \ .\]

Even more explicitly, the condition \(\text{det}\left( b^{m}_{\ \ j} - \kappa_{(\alpha)} \delta^{m}_{\ \ j} \right) = 0\) becomes

\[\begin{split}0 = \left| \begin{bmatrix} b^{1}_{\ \ 1} - \kappa_{(\alpha)} & b^{1}_{\ \ 2} \\ b^{2}_{\ \ 1} & b^{2}_{\ \ 2} - \kappa_{(\alpha)} \end{bmatrix} \right| = \kappa_{(\alpha)}^2 - \left( b^{1}_{\ \ 1} + b^{2}_{\ \ 2} \right) \kappa_{(\alpha)} + b^1_{\ \ 1} b^{2}_{\ \ 2} - b^{1}_{\ \ 2} b^{2}_{\ \ 1} = \kappa^2_{(\alpha)} - \text{tr}\{ \mathbf{B} \} \kappa_{(\alpha)} + \text{det}\{ \mathbf{B} \}\ .\end{split}\]

The eigenvalues are

\[\begin{aligned} \kappa_{(\alpha)} & = \frac{ \text{tr}\left\{ \mathbf{B} \right\} }{2} \mp \sqrt{ \left( \frac{ \text{tr}\left\{ \mathbf{B} \right\} }{2} \right)^2 - \text{det}\{ \mathbf{B} \} } \ . \end{aligned}\]

with \(\mathbf{B}\) the matrix collecting the mixed components of the curvature tensor, \([\mathbf{B}]_{ij} = \left[ b^{i}_{\ \ j} \right]\). It’s easy to prove that

\[\text{tr}\{ \mathbf{B} \} = \kappa_{1} + \kappa_2 \ ,\]

and

\[\text{det}\{ \mathbf{B} \} = \kappa_{1} \cdot \kappa_2 \ .\]

Mean curvature

The mean curvature is defined as the arithmetic average of the principal curvatures, \(H := \frac{1}{2} \left( \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 \right)\).

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} H & = \frac{1}{2} \left( \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 \right) = \\ & =\frac{ \text{tr} \{ [ b^{i}_{\ \ j} ] \} }{2} = \frac{1}{2} b^{i}_{\ \ i} = \\ & = \frac{1}{2} g^{ij} b_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( g^{11} b_{11} + g^{12} b_{12} + g^{21} b_{21} + g^{22} b_{22} \right) = \\ & = \frac{1}{2} [ g_{ij} ]^{-1} b_{ij} = \frac{1}{2 \left( g_{11} g_{22} - g_{12}^2 \right)} \left( g_{22} b_{11} - g_{12} b_{12} - g_{21} b_{21} + g_{11} b_{22} \right) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]
Gaussian curvature

Gaussian curvature is defined as the determinant of \(b^{i}_{\ \ j}\),

\[K = \text{det} \{ b^{i}_{\ \ j} \} = \dfrac{\text{tr}\{ b_{ij} \}}{\text{tr}\{ g_{ij} \}} \ ,\]

as \(b_{ij} = g_{il} b^{l}_{\ \ j}\).

9.2.4.1.1. Principal Curvatures and Directions#

The extreme values of the normal curvature \(\kappa_n = \frac{b_{ij}dq^i dq^j}{g_{ij}dq^i dq^j}\) are the principal curvatures \(\kappa_1\) and \(\kappa_2\), which are the eigenvalues of the tensor \(b^i_j\):

\[\det(b^i_j - \kappa \delta^i_j) = 0 \quad \implies \quad \kappa^2 - 2H\kappa + K = 0 .\]

Thus, the principal curvatures satisfy:

\[\kappa_{1,2} = H \pm \sqrt{H^2 - K} .\]

  • The Gaussian curvature is the product of the principal curvatures: \(K = \kappa_1 \kappa_2\).

  • The Mean curvature is the average of the principal curvatures: \(H = \frac{1}{2}(\kappa_1 + \kappa_2)\).

9.2.5. Fundamental forms and Gauss egregium theorem#

First and second fundamental forms arieses when dealing with vector quantities of a surface. Let \(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\) be a parametrization of the surface, here \((q^1, q^2), (u, v)\).

  • The differential, i.e. an elementary vectors on the surface, contains the first derivative of the parametrization and reads \(d \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b}_i d q^i = \mathbf{r}_i d q^i = \mathbf{r}_u du + \mathbf{r}_v dv\),

  • the covariant components of the metric tensor reads \(g_{ij} = \mathbf{b}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j\)

  • the first fundamental form appears in the legnth of an elementary vector on the surface,

    \[|d \mathbf{r}|^2 = d \mathbf{r} \cdot d \mathbf{r} = g_{uu} du^2 + 2 g_{uv} du dv + g_{vv} dv^2 = E du^2 + 2 F du dv + G dv^2 \ ,\]

    having introduced the common symbols \(E\), \(F\), \(G\).

  • the derivitves of the vectors of the natural basis induced by the parametrization, i.e. the second derivative of the manifold, are

    \[\partial_{k} \mathbf{b}_i = \partial_{ki} \mathbf{r} =: \Gamma_{ik}^\alpha \mathbf{b}_\alpha \ ,\]

    with \(i\), \(k \in \{ 1 , 2 \}\), \(\alpha \in \{ 1, 2, 3 \}\), and \(\mathbf{b}_3 = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\).

  • The derivatives of the elements of the first form w.r.t. the coordinates are - as \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{r}_u = \hat{\mathbf{n}} \cdot \mathbf{r}_v = 0\),

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} E_u & = 2 \mathbf{r}_{uu} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{u} && = 2 \Gamma_{11}^{1} g_{11} + 2 \Gamma_{11}^2 g_{12} \\ E_v & = 2 \mathbf{r}_{uv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{u} && = 2 \Gamma_{12}^{1} g_{11} + 2 \Gamma_{12}^2 g_{12} \\ F_u & = \mathbf{r}_{uu} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} + \mathbf{r}_{u} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{vu} && = \Gamma_{11}^{1} g_{12} + \Gamma_{11}^2 g_{22} + \Gamma_{12}^{1} g_{11} + \Gamma_{12}^2 g_{12} \\ F_v & = \mathbf{r}_{uv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} + \mathbf{r}_{u} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{vv} && = \Gamma_{12}^{1} g_{12} + \Gamma_{12}^2 g_{22} + \Gamma_{22}^{1} g_{11} + \Gamma_{22}^2 g_{12} \\ G_u & = 2 \mathbf{r}_{vu} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} && = 2 \Gamma_{21}^{1} g_{12} + 2 \Gamma_{21}^2 g_{22} \\ G_v & = 2 \mathbf{r}_{vv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} && = 2 \Gamma_{22}^{1} g_{12} + 2 \Gamma_{22}^2 g_{22} \\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    and thus

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}_{uu} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{u} & = \Gamma_{11}^{1} g_{11} + \Gamma_{11}^2 g_{12} && = \frac{1}{2} E_u \\ \mathbf{r}_{uu} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} & = \Gamma_{11}^{1} g_{12} + \Gamma_{11}^2 g_{22} && = F_u - \frac{1}{2} E_v \\ \mathbf{r}_{uv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{u} & = \Gamma_{12}^{1} g_{11} + \Gamma_{12}^2 g_{12} && = \frac{1}{2} E_v \\ \mathbf{r}_{uv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} & = \Gamma_{21}^{1} g_{12} + \Gamma_{21}^2 g_{22} && = \frac{1}{2} G_u \\ \mathbf{r}_{vv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{u} & = \Gamma_{22}^{1} g_{11} + \Gamma_{22}^2 g_{12}&& = F_v - \frac{1}{2} G_u \\ \mathbf{r}_{vv} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{v} & = \Gamma_{22}^{1} g_{12} + \Gamma_{22}^2 g_{22} && = \frac{1}{2} G_v \\ \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    Each pair of equations can be written as

    \[\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix} E & F \\ F & G \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Gamma_{a b}^{1} \\ \Gamma_{a b}^{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dots \\ \dots \end{bmatrix} \ ,\end{split}\]

    i.e., if the matrix is non-singular, i.e. if \(\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v \ne \mathbf{0}\), then Christoffel symbols can be written in terms of the coefficients of the first form and their derivatives.

  • the second fundamental form appears in the second-order derivative of the manifold. With an extrinsic approach, with the 2-dimensional surface embedded in a 3-dimensional space,

    \[\partial_{k} \mathbf{b}_i = \partial_{ki} \mathbf{r} =: \Gamma_{ik}^\alpha \mathbf{b}_\alpha \ ,\]

    with \(i\), \(k\) running on \(\{ 1, 2\}\), while \(\alpha\) running on \(\{ 1, 2, 3 \}\) with the third component identifying the normal direction. The second-order local approximation of the surface reads

    \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}(q^k+d q^k) & \sim \mathbf{r}(q^k) + d q^i \, \mathbf{r}_i(q^k) + \frac{1}{2} dq^i dq^j \mathbf{r}_{ij}(q^k) = \\ & \sim \mathbf{r}(q^k) + d q^i \, \mathbf{b}_i + \frac{1}{2} dq^i dq^j \Gamma_{ij}^{\alpha} \mathbf{b}_{\alpha} \ , \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

    so that the projection normal to the plane is

    \[\mathbf{\hat{n}} \cdot \left( \mathbf{r}(q^k+dq^k) - \mathbf{r}(q^k) \right) = \frac{1}{2} dq^i dq^j \Gamma^3_{ij} \ .\]
